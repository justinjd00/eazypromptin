Good, that's enough to lock most of it down. Here are my decisions on the open points, two things you need to know before starting, and three last questions.

## The big one: how you two actually connect

"Friends in other cities" means internet play, and the annoying part of internet play isn't the game code. It's getting two PCs behind home routers to see each other at all. Godot's built-in multiplayer (ENet) needs one of you to be reachable by IP, and home routers block that by default.

Your options:

- **Port forwarding** on the host's router. Works, but it's fiddly, some ISPs (especially with DS-Lite / CGNAT) make it impossible, and it's a pain to debug.
- **Steam networking** (via Facepunch.Steamworks or Steamworks.NET in C#). Solves it properly with lobbies, invites and relay servers. You can develop with Valve's test app ID 480 without paying anything. But it adds a big dependency and complexity on day one.
- **A virtual LAN like Tailscale (or ZeroTier).** Both of you install it once, you get a fixed private IP for each other, and the game just connects by IP with plain ENet. Free, zero code, works through basically any router.

**My call for v1: ENet + Tailscale.** It's just for you two, so there's no reason to fight networking infrastructure before the game is even fun. The one thing to insist on: the networking code sits behind a small "transport" layer (host / join / disconnect), so if you go to Steam later, you swap in Steam's transport and lobbies without touching gameplay code.

## Network architecture (this goes in the brief, it's what Claude Code most needs to get right)

- **Host-authoritative, listen server.** One of you hosts and plays, the other joins. No dedicated server.
- **The host owns everything that matters:** enemies, AI, spawning, damage, health, loot drops, upgrade rolls, room generation.
- **Each player owns their own movement.** Your client moves your character immediately and syncs the position. Technically that's "cheatable", but between two friends that doesn't matter, and it means movement feels instant even with 50ms+ of lag. This is the single biggest "does it feel good online" decision.
- **Attacks:** the client plays the swing/shot animation instantly, the host decides what actually got hit. Small mismatches are invisible at this scale.
- **One shared random seed per run,** sent by the host, so dungeon layout is identical on both machines without syncing every tile.
- Godot tools: `MultiplayerSpawner`, `MultiplayerSynchronizer`, and `[Rpc]` methods. All of these work fine in C#.

## C#: fine, two heads-ups

C# is a reasonable choice since you know it. Just know:
- You need the **.NET build of Godot 4** (separate download) and the .NET 8 SDK. The standard Godot build can't run C#.
- Most tutorials and forum answers are GDScript. Translating is easy, but tell Claude Code explicitly "C# only, no GDScript files", or it may mix them.

## Gameplay decisions

**Camera and combat: top-down 2D, real-time, twin-stick style.** WASD to move, mouse to aim, click to attack, a dodge roll on space. Gamepad support too, since that's cheap to add with Godot's input map. Top-down is much easier than a side-on platformer for co-op (no one falling off screen, simpler collision, easier to see each other).

**Run structure, roughly 20 minutes:**
- 3 floors, each with its own look and enemy set (e.g. mossy ruins, crystal cave, candy/mushroom forest to keep it bright), and a boss at the end of each floor.
- Each floor is about 6 to 8 rooms. Rooms are hand-made templates that get shuffled and connected randomly per run. Doors lock until the room is cleared.
- Room types: combat, treasure, shop, a rest/heal room, boss.

**Getting stronger within a run (this is what makes runs feel different):**
- Clearing rooms gives XP. On level-up, each player picks **1 of 3 random upgrades**, independently. So you two naturally build differently.
- Upgrades should combine, not just add numbers. "+10% damage" is boring. "Attacks bounce to a second enemy" plus "bounced hits burn" is a build. Aim for around 30 upgrades in the pool for v1, a handful of which explicitly synergise.
- A couple of **co-op upgrades** that only make sense with two players (e.g. "heal your partner when you dodge through them").
- 2 or 3 starting weapons (sword, bow, magic staff) that play differently.

**Co-op death rule:** when you hit 0 HP you go down, your partner can revive you by standing next to you for a few seconds. Both down means the run ends. This creates the best co-op moments for basically no extra complexity.

**Between runs, no grinding:** permanent progress unlocks *variety*, not raw power. Beating floor 2 the first time adds new upgrades to the pool, a boss kill unlocks a new starting weapon, that kind of thing. Maybe one or two tiny stat perks at most. That way a run is never "too hard until you farm".

## Art

Use **one** asset pack so it looks consistent. My pick: **"Ninja Adventure" by Pixel-boy** (free, CC0, on itch.io). Top-down, 16x16, bright and cute, and it comes with characters, monsters, tilesets, effects, UI and even music and sound effects. That covers almost everything for v1. Kenney's "Tiny Dungeon" is the backup if you prefer an even simpler look. Mixing packs is how hobby games end up looking messy.

## What v1 means

v1 is "a full run you two can actually play together and want to play again." It does **not** include Steam, menus beyond host/join, settings screens, save files beyond the unlock list, or more than one character model.

And it gets built in this order, each step playable before moving on:
1. Two players connect over Tailscale and both run around in one room. (Test locally first: Godot can launch 2 instances at once under Debug > Customize Run Instances.)
2. Combat: attacking, one enemy type, damage, downed and revive. All working over the network.
3. Room-to-room: doors, room clearing, a seeded floor layout.
4. XP and the 1-of-3 upgrade picks.
5. Content: more enemies, the 3 floors, bosses, weapons, the rest of the upgrades.
6. Unlocks between runs.

Step 1 and 2 are where online co-op projects usually break. If those feel good with real lag between your two cities, the rest is "just" content.
